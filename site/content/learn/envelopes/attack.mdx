Chapter 1 left you with a tone that began when you pressed Play. This one begins
when you press a key, and how quickly it gets there is the only thing on this
page. The same widget grows a slider over the next two lessons; this is it with
one.

<Patch id="envelopes/adsr" show={["attack"]} />

<Try>

With **Attack** at the bottom, play a key — with the pointer, or with the letter
keys on your computer keyboard. The note is at full loudness the instant you
touch it. Whatever that is, it was struck or plucked.

</Try>

<Try>

Drag **Attack** up to a second and play the same key. Now the note has to be
waited for. Nothing about the tone has changed — same oscillator, same pitch,
the same harmonics in the same proportions — and yet you would call it bowed or
blown rather than struck. Watch the contour under the keys draw the ramp.

</Try>

<Try>

Work in the first tenth of a second, where the slider is most sensitive.
Somewhere around ten milliseconds the attack stops being a shape you can hear
the start of and becomes an edge; below that you are hearing a click on the
front of the note rather than the note beginning.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

The first fifty milliseconds of a note carry more of its identity than all the
rest. Cut the beginning off a recorded piano and play someone the remainder and
the instrument becomes genuinely hard to name: the hammer, the key thump and the
fact that the harmonics do not all arrive at the same moment are in that
fragment and nowhere else.

Which is also why an attack of zero sounds artificial rather than merely fast.
Energy takes time to get into a real resonator, so nothing outside a
loudspeaker ever goes from silence to full amplitude in one sample — and a jump
like that is a step, whose spectrum is a click at every frequency at once. The
click you can hear at the bottom of the slider is that step, and it is not the
envelope.

</Aside>
