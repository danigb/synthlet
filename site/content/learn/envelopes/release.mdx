Attack, decay and sustain all happen while the key is down. The fourth stage
starts the moment it comes up, which makes release the one part of the contour
your hands time directly — and the one the reader of a patch sheet always
underestimates.

<Patch id="envelopes/adsr" show={["attack", "decay", "sustain", "release"]} />

<Try>

**Release** at the bottom, **Sustain** near the top. Play a note and let go. The
sound stops the way a light switch stops, and there is a small click as it goes:
the same click the attack slider had at its own end of the contour, for the same
reason.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **Release** up to two seconds and play something short. The tail is now
longer than the note that caused it, and each note is still leaving while the
next arrives. That is most of what a pad is, and it is one slider.

</Try>

<Try>

Put **Sustain** at zero with a fast **Decay**, hold a key until it is silent,
then let go. The release does nothing you can hear. It does not begin at the
sustain level; it begins wherever the contour has got to, and the contour was
already on the floor.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

An instant ending is not silence arriving, it is a step in the waveform, and a
step contains every frequency at once — which is why it reads as a click and not
as a stop. Give the amplifier even twenty milliseconds to close and the step
becomes a ramp, the broadband splash goes with it, and the ear hears the note
ending instead of something breaking.

That is also the first hint of a rule this chapter keeps running into: the
envelope is not decoration on the sound, it is part of the signal. A contour
with a corner in it puts that corner into the spectrum.

</Aside>
