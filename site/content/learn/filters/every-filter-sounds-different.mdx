Reid ends his second filter article by asserting that every analogue filter
design sounds different from every other, and then has to leave it there,
because a magazine cannot hand you a Moog and a Korg and an Oberheim on the same
page. This page can.

<Patch id="filters/shootout" show={["model", "cutoff", "resonance", "drive"]} />

<Try>

Press Play and walk down **Model** while the note holds. Nothing else changes —
same oscillator, same cutoff, same resonance — and the switch is a ten
millisecond crossfade, so there is no gap to hide a difference in. The ladder is
thick and squashes the bass as the resonance rises; the Korg 35 bites; the diode
ladder is soft and blurred; the Oberheim is clean and even.

</Try>

<Try>

Leave the model on **Moog ladder** and watch where the trace turns against the
mark, then switch to **Oberheim low-pass**. The mark is the frequency you asked
for and the knee is where the circuit actually put it. Ask for 1 kHz and the
ladder answers around 907 Hz, the half ladder 1163, the Korg 35 870, the diode
ladder 502 and the Oberheim 1542. The
[measurements](/docs/vaf) are in the documentation, and every one of them is
asserted in the filter's own test suite.

</Try>

<Try>

Set **Resonance** to 0.9 and walk the list again. This is where the circuits
diverge most: two of them will sing on their own past 0.95 and the rest never
will, because only the two ladders have a saturating feedback path to hold the
oscillation steady.

</Try>

<Try>

Put the resonance back to 0.7 and push **Drive** up. Seven of the nine have a
real nonlinearity for it to reach, so the filter distorts from the inside rather
than after the fact. On the two Korg 35s, which are linear by design, it is
nothing but input gain.

</Try>

<Epigraph source="Gordon Reid, Synth Secrets Part 6">

Indeed, if you take the output from a Moog oscillator and pass it through a Korg
filter, the result sounds like... a Korg synthesizer.

</Epigraph>
