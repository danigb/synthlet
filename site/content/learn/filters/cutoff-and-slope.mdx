Two numbers describe a low-pass filter, and almost everyone gets the first one
wrong. The <Term>cutoff frequency</Term> is not the frequency above which the
filter starts working — it is defined as the frequency at which the signal has
already been attenuated by three decibels, which the ear hears perfectly well.
The second number is the <Term>slope</Term>: how fast it keeps falling after
that.

<Patch id="filters/slope" show={["cutoff", "slope"]} />

<Try>

Press Play and drag **Cutoff** from 16 kHz down to a few hundred hertz. The
vertical mark is the frequency you asked for. Watch the trace rather than the
line: it is already bending downwards well below the mark, and the harmonic
sitting exactly under it is noticeably quieter than its neighbour below. That is
the three decibels, and it is why a filter set to 1 kHz sounds darker than you
would expect from the number.

</Try>

<Try>

Park the cutoff around 500 Hz, where there is plenty of sawtooth above it, and
step **Slope** through the three. The 24 dB per octave ladder cuts the top off
like a wall; the two 12 dB filters leave a hill of harmonics running away above
the corner. The difference is not subtle, and it is the whole reason a synth's
specification sheet bothers to print the number.

</Try>

<Try>

Now compare only the first two, which fall at the same rate. They still do not
sound alike, and their knees do not sit in the same place against the mark.
Slope is one property of a filter and not the filter.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Filters are counted in poles: one pole is 6 dB per octave, two is 12, four is 24. You cannot get there by chaining four simple circuits, because they load
each other and stop behaving; real designs put amplifiers between the stages,
and even then the four corners never land exactly on top of one another. Reid's
conclusion is the useful one: a four-pole filter tends towards 24 dB per octave
a long way above the corner, but on the way there it passes through regions that
fall at 6, at 12 and at 18.

</Aside>
