Almost every physical object has frequencies it vibrates at naturally. A passive
filter has none — it only passes or attenuates. But an active circuit can send
part of its output back to its own input, and then there is a frequency at which
that loop reinforces itself. That is <Term>resonance</Term>, and the frequency is
the corner.

<Patch id="filters/resonance" show={["resonance", "cutoff", "source"]} />

<Try>

Play a few keys with **Resonance** near 0.7 and then push it towards 0.9. A peak
grows on the mark, the harmonic nearest the corner gets louder than it was going
in, and the sawtooth acquires a hollow, vocal quality. Everything far above and
far below the corner gets quieter at the same time; the filter is concentrating
the sound rather than only removing from it.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **Resonance** past 0.95 and turn **Source** off. The sawtooth is gone and
there is still a note — a pure sine wave, generated by the filter with nothing
going into it. Play the keyboard: the cutoff follows each key, so the filter is
now an oscillator and the keys are in tune across both octaves.

</Try>

<Try>

With the filter still singing, drag **Cutoff** by hand. It is the same parameter
the keys write, so the slider overrides the tracking until the next key press —
which is exactly what a pitch knob on an oscillator does. Then bring **Source**
back on and hear both at once: a sawtooth with a sine riding on top of it.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

A linear feedback loop has one gain for every amplitude, so it can only decay,
hold, or run away. What makes a Moog ladder settle into a steady sine instead is
that its feedback path saturates: as the amplitude grows the loop gain falls,
and it balances. The corner is also not quite where you asked for it. A cascade
of four one-pole sections crosses its 3 dB point below its design frequency — ask
this ladder for 1000 Hz and it resonates near 907 — so the patch divides by that
ratio before setting the cutoff. Without that division the filter sings in tune
with itself but more than a tone flat of the key you pressed, which is what the
filter-tracking trimmer on the front of an analogue synth was there to fix.

</Aside>
