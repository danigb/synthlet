---
title: "Filters"
description: "What a filter actually does to a sound, why the cutoff knob is not where you think it is, and the one page where nine analogue circuits meet the same sawtooth."
---

A filter is the part of a synthesiser that takes something away, and it is the
part people buy an instrument for. This chapter spends six pages on it because
the simple account — a knob that makes the top end quieter — is wrong in three
separate ways, and each of the three is audible the moment someone shows you
where to listen.

It opens with phase rather than with a cutoff knob, which is how Gordon Reid
opens his. A filter is a phase device before it is a volume device, and the
cheapest way to prove that is to take a signal, add a copy of itself five
milliseconds late, and watch a row of holes appear in the spectrum without a
filter anywhere in the patch. From there the chapter earns the ordinary knobs:
where the corner really sits, how fast the slope falls away from it, which side
of the corner is kept, and what happens when the corner is fed back into itself
hard enough that the filter starts singing on its own.

The last page is the one no book could print. Reid could only assert that every
analogue filter design sounds different from every other; here that assertion is
a select box. The same sawtooth, the same cutoff, and nine circuits — a Moog
ladder, a half ladder, two Korg 35s, a diode ladder and four taps of an Oberheim
— plus the library's clean digital filter for contrast. Switch between them
while a note is held and the claim stops being a claim.
