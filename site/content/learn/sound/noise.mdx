Everything so far has had a fundamental with a series of harmonics above it,
because everything so far has been a one-dimensional oscillator: a string, or a
column of air in a tube. Hit a drum skin and none of it holds.

<Patch id="sound/noise" show={["type", "filter", "cutoff"]} />

<Try>

Press Play with **Type** on **White**. The spectrum is flat from one end to the
other: every frequency at once, in equal measure. There is no pitch to hear
because there is no peak to hear it at.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **Pink**. The spectrum tilts — about three decibels down per octave —
and the hiss drops from a cymbal to something nearer rain or a distant motorway.
Pink has equal energy per octave rather than per hertz, and octaves are how your
ear divides the spectrum up, so pink is the one that sounds even to a human.

</Try>

<Try>

Turn **Filter** on and bring **Cutoff** down slowly. Around 6 kHz you have a
hi-hat, around 1 kHz a snare, below 200 Hz wind. Then tap **Play** twice in
quick succession at each of those settings: a short burst of filtered noise is
already most of a drum, and the shape of the burst is what chapter 2 is about.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

A string is a line and a drum skin is a surface, and that one extra dimension is
the whole difference. The places a skin can hold still are not the simple
fractions a string's are; they fall where a Bessel function crosses zero, so the
second mode of an ideal drum sits at 2.296 times the fundamental instead of 2,
and the third at 3.6 instead of 3. Keep going up and the modes become so many and
so closely spaced that the ear stops trying to find a pitch among them. A noise
generator is the cheap way to arrive at that state directly — which is why the
Minimoog's drum patch has all three oscillators off and only the noise switched
on.

</Aside>
