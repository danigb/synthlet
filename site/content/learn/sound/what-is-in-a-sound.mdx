<Epigraph source="Gordon Reid, Synth Secrets, Part 1">

The natures of musical tones are defined by the numbers and amplitudes of the
harmonics contained within them.

</Epigraph>

A sound is air pressure changing over time. Any change you can hear — a violin,
a voice, a door — can be written as a stack of sine waves: one at the pitch you
hear, and a series of quieter ones above it at whole-number multiples of it.
Those upper ones are the <Term>harmonics</Term>, and which of them are present,
and how loud each one is, is the whole of what we call <Term>timbre</Term>.

A sawtooth is the honest case: every harmonic present, the nth one at 1/n of the
fundamental's amplitude. Nothing missing, nothing favoured.

## Build one

The widget below is an oscillator whose wavetable is rebuilt from that list
every time you move the first knob. At 1 it is a single sine: one frequency, no
harmonics, the sound a tuning fork makes and nothing else. Every step up adds
the next one at 1/n.

<Patch id="sound/harmonics" show={["harmonics", "cutoff", "strip"]} />

<Try>

Start at 1 and go up one harmonic at a time. Listen for the point where it stops
sounding like a tone and starts sounding like an instrument — for most people it
is somewhere around 4 or 5, long before the knob runs out.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Harmonics are not a convention of music, they are a property of things that
vibrate. A string fixed at both ends can only hold waves with a node at each
end, which means half a wavelength, or one, or one and a half — the whole-number
series and nothing in between. A column of air in a tube is the same argument
with different boundaries. That is why the series is whole numbers everywhere,
and why two instruments playing the same note share a skeleton.

</Aside>

## Take it apart again

Now the other direction. The Cutoff knob is a filter: it passes what is below it
and removes what is above. Put the harmonics back up to 16, then bring the
cutoff down.

<Try>

Sweep the cutoff from the top to the bottom and back. Nothing about the pitch
changes — the fundamental is there the whole time — but the sound goes from
bright to dull to almost a sine again. Then switch on **Strip harmonics**, which
is the same move made as a switch: everything above the fifth, gone.

</Try>

You have now done both halves of the course. Adding harmonics to build a tone is

<Term>additive synthesis</Term>; starting with all of them and taking some away
is <Term>subtractive synthesis</Term>, which is what almost every synthesiser
you will ever meet does, and what the rest of these chapters are about.

<Aside kind="history">

The two were rival architectures for a decade. Additive won the argument on
paper — any sound at all, given enough oscillators — and lost it on cost: a
convincing tone wanted dozens of them with their own envelopes, and the Hammond
organ's ninety-one tonewheels are what that costs in 1935. One oscillator and
one filter gets most of the way there for the price of two modules, which is why
subtractive is the shape of the Minimoog, the Prophet, and the synth in your
browser.

</Aside>

</Aside>
