<Epigraph source="Gordon Reid, Synth Secrets, Part 1">

The natures of musical tones are defined by the numbers and amplitudes of the
harmonics contained within them.

</Epigraph>

A sound is air pressure changing over time, and any change you can hear — a
violin, a voice, a door — is a stack of sine waves: one at the pitch you hear,
and quieter ones above it at whole-number multiples of it. Those upper ones are
the <Term>harmonics</Term>, and which are present, and how loud each is, is the
whole of what we call <Term>timbre</Term>.

A sawtooth is the honest case: every harmonic present, the nth at 1/n of the
fundamental.

<Try>

Start at 1 and climb one harmonic at a time. Listen for where it stops sounding
like a tone and starts sounding like an instrument — for most people around 4 or
5, long before the knob runs out.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Harmonics are not a convention of music, they are a property of things that
vibrate. A string fixed at both ends can only hold waves with a node at each
end — half a wavelength, or one, or one and a half — which is the whole-number
series and nothing in between. A tube of air is the same argument with different
boundaries, which is why two instruments playing the same note share a
skeleton.

</Aside>

Now the other direction. **Strip harmonics** is a filter: it keeps what is below
it and removes what is above, set here to cut above the fifth.

<Try>

Put the harmonics up to 16, then switch **Strip harmonics** on and off. The
pitch does not change — the fundamental is there throughout — but the sound goes
from bright to dull, and the eleven marks above the fifth lose what stood under
them.

</Try>

You have now done both halves of the course. Building a tone by adding harmonics
is <Term>additive synthesis</Term>; starting with all of them and taking some
away is <Term>subtractive synthesis</Term>, which is what the rest of these
chapters are about.

<Aside kind="history">

Additive won the argument on paper — any sound at all, given enough oscillators —
and lost it on cost: the Hammond organ's ninety-one tonewheels are what "enough"
meant in 1935. One oscillator and one filter get most of the way there for the
price of two, which is the shape of almost every synth since.

</Aside>
