The last page used the waveform list as a way of changing timbre. This page is
about the list itself, because a waveform's name is shorthand for a set of
harmonics, and learning which name means which set is most of learning to guess
how a sound was made.

<Patch id="sound/waveforms" show={["waveform", "width"]} />

<Try>

Press Play and step down the list. **Sine** has nothing above the fundamental at
all: a tuning fork, or a flute played gently. **Triangle** is nearly that, with
a trace of an edge on it. **Sawtooth** has every harmonic at 1/n and is the
brightest of the four — it is the raw material of brass and of strings.
**Square** has the odd harmonics only, which is the hollow, woody sound a
clarinet makes.

</Try>

<Try>

Leave it on **Square** and drag **Width** down from the middle. Dashed lines
appear, and they mark the harmonics that should be _missing_: at a width of 0.5
they land on every second one, at 0.33 on every third, at 0.25 on every fourth.
The sound goes from hollow to reedy to thin as the holes crowd together, and the
holes are where the lines are.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Reid's rule for pulse waves is worth memorising: a pulse has the same harmonics
as a sawtooth, except that for a duty cycle of 1:n, every nth harmonic is
missing. The square wave is just the case n = 2 — which means "a square wave has
only odd harmonics", a thing people quote and rarely explain, is not a separate
fact at all. Widths between the whole ratios do not remove harmonics, they only
attenuate them, which is why the sound changes smoothly under your finger rather
than in steps.

</Aside>

Width does something to a triangle too, and nothing at all to a sine or a
sawtooth. The oscillator treats it as the position of the wave's second corner,
so a triangle with its corner pushed all the way to one end has become a
sawtooth — which is a good demonstration that these four names are points on a
continuum rather than four separate devices.
