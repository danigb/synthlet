Almost every book on synthesis opens by saying that a sound has three
qualities — <Term>pitch</Term>, <Term>loudness</Term> and <Term>timbre</Term> —
and that a synthesiser has an oscillator for the first, an amplifier for the
second and a filter for the third. Reid spends sixty-three parts complicating that, and he is
right to. But it is a useful simplification, and this page is where you can
check it: one oscillator, three knobs, and each knob moves exactly one thing.

<Patch id="sound/tone" show={["frequency", "volume", "waveform"]} />

The dashed lines on the spectrum are a prediction — the nth harmonic is at n
times the fundamental — drawn over the measurement so you can watch the two
agree.

<Try>

Sweep **Frequency**. The lines and the peaks climb together and spread apart as
they go, because the gap between harmonics _is_ the fundamental and it is
growing. The shape on the scope never changes; it only gets narrower.

</Try>

<Try>

Sweep **Volume**. Now nothing moves sideways at all: the whole spectrum slides
up and down, and the scope's trace gets taller and shorter. Loudness is the one
quality of the three that changes no frequency.

</Try>

<Try>

Step through **Waveform** with the other two left alone. Sine puts a peak under
the first line and leaves the other seven bare. Triangle and square fill the
odd-numbered lines and skip the even ones. Sawtooth fills all of them. Same
pitch, same loudness, four different instruments.

</Try>

<Aside kind="note">

The volume knob is marked in decibels because that is what your ear counts in:
every 6 dB is a halving, so the quiet end of the travel is where most of the
useful range lives. The parameter underneath is a plain multiplier — the control
does the conversion, which is why a patch never has to.

</Aside>

That last one is the interesting knob, and it is the reason the next page exists:
"waveform" is not a shape so much as a recipe, and the four names are four
recipes worth knowing by ear.
