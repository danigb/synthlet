Dennis Gabor argued in 1947 that any sound can be described as a stream of very
short fragments, each windowed so it starts and stops without a click. Iannis
Xenakis turned the argument into music in the 1960s. The useful part, for a
synthesist, is what falls out of the description: once a sound is a cloud of
fragments, the number of them, how long each one is, what pitch each one is
played at and where each one came from are four independent quantities.

The widget granulates a clip. The clip is the same one the documentation uses,
and the module is running fully wet, so everything you hear is grains.

<Try>

Press Play, then take **Rate** down to five or six grains a second. You can hear
them individually: a stutter, with silence between. Now run it back up past a
hundred and they overlap into a continuous texture. **Duration** is separate, so
try short grains at a high rate and then long ones at a low rate — the same two
numbers make quite different objects.

</Try>

<Try>

Turn **Spray** up. Each grain now reaches back a random distance for its
material instead of taking the freshest, so the ordering of the source dissolves
while its character survives. Then raise **Pitch spread**: the grains stop
agreeing about what note they are, and a recognisable clip becomes a chord of
itself.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch **Freeze** on. Nothing new is written into the buffer, so the last few
seconds become an object you can walk around in. With **Feedback** up, grains
are summed back in and granulated again, which is how a short freeze becomes a
texture that keeps evolving.

</Try>

<Aside kind="note">

Every per-grain quantity here is a centre and a spread, drawn once per grain.
With every spread at zero the module is deterministic and every grain is
identical; the spreads are what turn a stream into a cloud. That pairing is
Barry Truax's control model, and it is why there is no single "randomness" knob.

</Aside>
