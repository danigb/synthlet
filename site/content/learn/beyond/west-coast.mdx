Everything in this tutorial so far has been East Coast synthesis. Start with a
harmonically rich waveform, take harmonics away with a filter, and shape the
result with an amplifier: oscillator, filter, amplifier, in that order, which is
Bob Moog's architecture and the reason the word _subtractive_ exists.

On the other side of the country, Don Buchla was building instruments at the
same time that answer the same question in the opposite direction. Start with a
sine wave, which has no harmonics at all, and _add_ them. The way you add them
is folding. Drive the sine into a circuit that reflects the waveform back on
itself every time it passes a threshold, and a single loud peak becomes several
smaller ones. The harder you drive it, the more folds there are and the more
harmonics appear — and unlike a filter sweep, the new partials arrive high in
the spectrum rather than being uncovered at the bottom. One knob, from a pure
tone to something metallic, with no filter anywhere.

The second half of the architecture is just as unfamiliar. Buchla had no
separate filter and amplifier at the end of the chain; he had a lowpass gate,
which is one circuit doing both jobs through a photoresistor. Send it a pulse
and the sound gets louder and brighter together, then gets quieter and darker
together as the cell relaxes, which is what a struck physical object does. That
relationship is why a lowpass gate sounds like wood rather than like an envelope.

Half of this ships today: the lowpass gate is an attack-decay envelope driving a
filter and an amplifier from one gate, which the library already has. The other
half does not exist. There is no wavefolder module in synthlet, and — unusually
for this tutorial — there is no ticket for one either, so this page is waiting on
a design as well as on code. A saturating clipper cannot stand in: saturation
rounds the peaks off, and folding turns them inside out.
