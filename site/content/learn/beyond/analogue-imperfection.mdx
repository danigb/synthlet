Reid explains in Part 46 why a cheap string machine sounds like an ensemble and
an expensive dual-oscillator polysynth does not. It is not the number of
oscillators. It is that no two of the imagined players are doing exactly the
same thing at exactly the same moment, and a synth with two perfectly stable
oscillators is two players who are, forever.

A digital oscillator is worse off than an analogue one here, because it really
is perfectly stable. The fix is to put the imperfection back as a parameter.

<Try>

Press Play and listen to the pair of oscillators for a moment: detuned, beating,
and absolutely steady. Now bring **Pitch chaos** up slowly. Each oscillator
starts wandering a few cents on its own, without reference to the other, and the
beating stops being a regular pulse and starts being movement.

</Try>

<Try>

Add **Level chaos**. Each cycle now arrives a little louder or quieter than the
last, which is the thing a bow or a set of bellows does and an amplifier does
not. A small amount is plenty; past halfway it stops being an instrument and
becomes a fault.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **Drift depth** to a few hundred hertz. The filter's corner now wanders,
driven by a shape that has a rate but no period — it never repeats, so you never
learn it. Then turn **Age** up on the delay at the end: the repeats lose their
top end and wobble, and the whole patch sounds like it has been switched on for
an hour rather than a second.

</Try>

<Aside kind="note">

Pitch chaos and level chaos are each two parameters on the module, not one: how
wild the deviation is, and how far it may reach. Either of them at zero means
silence from the other, so the knobs here write both at once. A control that
does nothing until a second control moves is a control nobody finds.

</Aside>
