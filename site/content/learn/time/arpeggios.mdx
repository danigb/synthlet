An arpeggiator takes a set of notes and walks it. That is the whole idea, and it
is old enough that the Roland Jupiter-4 and the Sequential Prophet-600 both had
one, and modern enough that a great deal of what people call a synth line is
nothing else.

What is unusual here is where it sits. This arpeggiator is a worklet and its
output is a **frequency**, in hertz, which goes straight into an oscillator.
There is no note event anywhere in the patch — nothing is scheduled, nothing is
counted, and the main thread is not involved once Play is pressed.

<Patch id="time/arp" show={["mode", "scale", "octaves", "baseNote", "bpm"]} />

<Try>

Press Play and change **Mode** while it runs. Up, down, up-and-down, and two
kinds of random: the first wanders and never repeats a note immediately, the
second plays every note of the set once per pass in a fresh order.

</Try>

<Try>

Change **Scale**. A chord is a short scale — the same parameter holds both,
because it is a twelve-bit mask of which pitch classes are allowed — so
**Triad minor** is an arpeggio in the guitarist's sense and **Dorian** is a scale
run, from one control.

</Try>

<Try>

Play the keyboard. The root moves and the pattern keeps walking: the traversal's
position lives in the worklet, so a new root arrives at the next step rather
than restarting the figure. Then take **Octaves** to four and hear how far it
climbs before it comes back.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Every hardware arpeggiator emits note messages for something else to turn into
pitch, which is why its chord has to be in your fingers. This one has no
note-event tier underneath it, so `scale`, `mode` and `baseNote` are ordinary
parameters — and a parameter can be automated. Send a slow sequence of masks to
`scale` and you have a chord progression; send an oscillator to `mode` and you
have an arpeggiator whose own direction is arpeggiated.

</Aside>
