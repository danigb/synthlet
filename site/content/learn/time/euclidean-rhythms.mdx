Spread three hits as evenly as you can over eight slots. There is no exact
answer — eight does not divide by three — and the best you can do turns out to
be a rhythm with a name. It is the tresillo, the left hand of half of Cuban
music, the bassline of a great deal of house, and the thing a Roland TR-808
programmer arrives at by ear in about a minute.

The algorithm that finds it is Euclid's, the one for greatest common divisors,
and Godfried Toussaint's observation is that running it on two integers
reproduces a startling number of the rhythms people actually play. Nothing below
is a pattern anyone typed in.

<Patch id="time/euclid" show={["steps", "beats", "rotation", "spread"]} />

<Try>

Press Play. Eight steps, three beats: the tresillo. Take **Beats** to five and it
becomes the cinquillo, its complement and its constant companion. Then try
sixteen steps with five beats, which is the bossa nova.

</Try>

<Try>

Move **Rotation**. The grid shifts but its shape does not: a necklace has no
beginning, so rotating it chooses where the downbeat falls among hits that were
already in that arrangement. Several of the named rhythms are the same necklace
entered in different places.

</Try>

<Try>

Move **Spread** and listen to the hi-hat rather than the kick. At zero the two
land together; at two they interlock. This is one pattern read from two
starting points, not two patterns.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

The fan is the part that is easy to miss. One necklace is several named rhythms
simultaneously, and those are the parts different players hold rather than
variations on one part — so the hi-hat here is not a second generator. Both
drums read one pattern array and one step counter, which is why they cannot
drift apart however long the widget runs, and why one reset aligns every
channel.

</Aside>
