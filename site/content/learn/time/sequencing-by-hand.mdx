An analogue step sequencer is sixteen knobs and a switch that walks them. Each
knob is a voltage, the switch hands one of them to an oscillator at a time, and
the result is the Moroder bassline, the Berlin School, and a large part of why
anyone bought a synthesiser with a sequencer attached.

This page builds one the way software normally does. An animation frame wakes
up, looks a quarter of a second into the future, and writes the next bar of
pitches and gates as scheduled parameter changes. The audio is still accurate to
the sample, because every value carries a time. The **arming** is not.

<Try>

Press Play and move a step. The number is in semitones above the root, so twelve
is an octave and seven a fifth. The change does not arrive immediately: the bar
it belongs to was armed before you touched it, and the sequencer reads the
sliders when it arms the next one.

</Try>

<Try>

Now stall the page. Open the developer console and run a loop that spins for two
or three seconds — anything that keeps JavaScript busy. The rhythm survives a
short stall, because a quarter of a second was already written; a longer one
arrives late, all at once. Go back to the clock and the arpeggiator two pages
ago and do the same thing. Neither of them notices.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

This is the one file in the chapter with a timer in it, and the difference is
worth stating plainly. A lookahead scheduler is not inaccurate — the samples
land where they were told to. It is **interruptible**: the window is a bet that
the main thread will come back before the music runs out of instructions, and
every other tab, every layout, every garbage collection is betting against you.
The clock, the rhythm generator and the arpeggiator make no such bet, because
nothing outside the audio thread is involved in keeping them going.

</Aside>
