The module from chapter 5 has not changed. It still closes a switch, catches
whatever voltage it finds and holds it until the next tick. The only difference
on this page is the rate: run it tens of thousands of times a second instead of
a few, and it is the front half of every converter ever built.

Everything strange about digital audio is already there in the module, which is
Reid's point in Part 17 and the reason the chapter arrives here rather than
starting here.

<Try>

Press Play. A 10 kHz sine, sampled at 13,333 Hz. The tallest peak in the
spectrum is not at either of those numbers — it is at 3,333 Hz, the difference
between them, and it is a tone that was never in the input: that is the effect
called <Term>aliasing</Term>. The three marks on the trace are half the sample
rate, the tone that was asked for, and where the arithmetic says the fold will
land.

</Try>

<Try>

Turn **Anti-alias filter** on. The intruder disappears, because the filter sits
before the switch and removes everything above half the sample rate before it
can be sampled. There is no way to remove it afterwards: two different input
frequencies genuinely produce the same sequence of samples, so nothing
downstream has anything to work with.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **Tone** down to about 500 Hz and look at the scope. You can count the
steps. Turn **Reconstruction filter** on and they round off into a sine — the
same filter, on the other side of the switch. Then drop **Bit depth** towards
four and hear the other kind of error: not the timing of the samples but how
finely each one was measured.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Half the sample rate is the Nyquist limit, and it is not a quality threshold —
it is the frequency above which the information is genuinely gone. A converter's
input filter exists to enforce it, and its output filter exists because a
staircase is a sine plus a great deal of high-frequency rubbish that the
sampling put there. Both filters are the same low-pass; only their position
differs.

</Aside>
