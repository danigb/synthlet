---
title: "Time as a signal"
description: "Clocks, rhythms and arpeggios that live inside the audio graph, and the three modules that turn one sound into a control voltage for another."
---

Parts 15 to 17 of Synth Secrets are about time, and they are not about
sequencers. Reid's subject is what happens when a control voltage carries a
shape rather than a setting: a voltage that rises and falls with the loudness of
a sound, a voltage that will not move faster than you allow it to, a voltage
sampled at intervals until the intervals themselves become the thing you hear.
Every module in this chapter reads one signal and answers with a slower one.

Three of the nine pages are things the book has no module for, because in 1999
there was no reason to want them. A clock, a Euclidean rhythm generator and an
arpeggiator all live **inside the graph** here: they are worklets, their outputs
are signals, and nothing on the page is counting for them. That is not a
performance trick. It means the tempo is accurate to the sample, that a stalled
browser tab cannot make a beat late, and that everything you already know about
patching applies to rhythm too — a clock can be scaled, an arpeggiator's mode
can be modulated, a rhythm can be transposed by a cable.

One page deliberately does it the other way — sixteen sliders, an animation
frame and a lookahead window, which is how a DAW keeps time — so that the
difference is something you can hear rather than something you are asked to
believe.

The chapter ends by running a sample-and-hold fast enough that it stops being a
modulation effect and becomes an analogue-to-digital converter, which is where
aliasing comes from.
