An envelope generator invents a shape. An envelope **follower** measures one. It
rectifies whatever it is given, charges a capacitor from the result and
discharges it slowly, and what comes out is a control voltage that rises and
falls with the loudness of the input. Reid's Figure 9 in Part 15 does exactly
one thing with it: it replaces the contour generator in an otherwise ordinary
patch, so the filter is opened by the sound rather than by the keyboard.

The signal being followed here is a piece of music rather than an instrument in
the patch, which is worth saying because it is the general case: a follower does
not care what it is listening to.

<Try>

Press Play with **Destination** on **Auto-wah**. The top trace is the follower's
output — the shape of the clip, a few times a second — and the mark on the
spectrum is where the filter's corner has been pushed to. Loud passages open it;
quiet ones close it. Nobody is moving anything.

</Try>

<Try>

Shorten **Attack** to nothing and lengthen **Release**. Each hit snaps the filter
open and it sags back slowly, which is the wah pedal everyone recognises. Then
do the opposite: a long attack ignores transients entirely and follows only the
shape of the phrase.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **Ducking**. The same follower, inverted and pointed at a pad's gain
instead of a cutoff, and the pad now gets out of the way on every hit. That is
the whole of a sidechain compressor, minus the marketing.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Attack and release here are not the envelope's, they are the detector's, and
they are the only two settings that matter. Too fast and the follower tracks
individual cycles of the waveform rather than its outline, which comes out as
distortion in the control voltage. Too slow and it misses the transients that
make the effect legible. The detector also has a choice of measurements: peak
answers with the loudest sample, which catches clicks, and RMS answers with
average power, which is what a compressor wants.

</Aside>
