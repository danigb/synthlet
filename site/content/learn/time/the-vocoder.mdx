A vocoder is the previous page done sixteen times at once. Split one sound — the
**modulator** — into bands with a row of band-pass filters. Follow the loudness
of each band. Split a second sound — the **carrier** — into the same bands, and
use those sixteen control voltages to open sixteen amplifiers, one per band. The
carrier comes out wearing the modulator's spectrum.

The modulator here is the site's music clip, not a voice, and that is not a
compromise worth hiding. A vocoder is indifferent to what it is given; what it
needs is a modulator whose spectrum **moves**. Speech moves more interestingly
than music, which is the entire reason the effect is famous, but the mechanism
is the same either way.

<Try>

Press Play. It takes a moment: there are 48 worklets in this patch — two filters
and a follower for each of sixteen bands — which is why this page is heavier
than the rest of the chapter. What you hear is a sawtooth being carved into the
shape of the clip.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **Bands** from sixteen to four. Four wide bands cannot describe a spectrum,
so what survives is a coarse impression of it, which is the robot everyone means
by the word. Sixteen is close enough to intelligible that you can follow the
music through it.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch **Carrier** to **Noise** and it becomes a whisper. A sawtooth has
harmonics at every multiple of its pitch, so the bands carve a pitched sound;
noise has energy everywhere and no pitch at all, so the same bands produce a
whispering voice with no note in it.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Fewer bands cannot simply mean switching some off. Four of the original sixteen
would be four narrow windows with silence between them, which is a comb filter
and not a coarse picture — so the bands that remain widen as the count drops.
That is the same trade a real vocoder makes in its filter design, and it is why
a twenty-band unit sounds articulate where an eight-band one sounds like a
machine.

</Aside>
