Chapter 5 sampled a noise generator once a beat and pointed the held voltage at
a cutoff. Point the same voltage at a **pitch** instead and the result is
famously unlistenable: every note between the notes, all of them equally likely,
none of them in any key.

Part 16 fixes it with one more module. A quantiser takes a fractional note
number and snaps it to the nearest member of a scale, so the randomness survives
and the wrong notes do not. Three modules and a cable, and the patch writes
melodies on its own.

<Patch id="time/random-arp" show={["scale", "root", "rate", "range"]} />

<Try>

Press Play. The scope is drawing note numbers rather than the sound: a staircase,
and every tread sits on a line the scale allows. Change **Scale** and watch the
available treads change with it — **Triad minor** has three per octave, **Blues**
six, **Whole tone** six evenly spaced.

</Try>

<Try>

Move **Range** down to two or three semitones. The melody collapses into a
wobble around one note, which is what a random voltage sounds like before the
quantiser has anything to choose between. Take it to twenty-four and it leaps
across two octaves.

</Try>

<Try>

Play the keyboard to move the centre note, and take **Rate** up past four
hundred. Somewhere above that the notes stop being a melody and become a texture,
which is where this patch stopped being an arpeggiator and became the sound on
half the progressive rock records of 1973.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

The quantiser is semitones in and hertz out, which is why nothing has to
interpret its answer: it goes straight into an oscillator. Getting the voltage
into its input is a scaling job — the held sample is minus one to one, so
multiplying by twelve and adding sixty is two octaves of note numbers around
middle C. A scale is a twelve-bit mask of allowed pitch classes and the key is a
separate number, so transposing the melody and changing its mode are two
different controls, as they are on paper.

</Aside>
