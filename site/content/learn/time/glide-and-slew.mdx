A slew limiter refuses to let its output move faster than you allow. Feed it a
step and it climbs; feed it a staircase and the corners round off. Point one at
a pitch and you have portamento, which is the effect on the front panel of every
monosynth since the Minimoog, and which is one module rather than a feature.

It has two rates, not one, and that is the interesting part. A rise time and a
fall time that differ turn a square input into a shape no envelope generator in
this library produces.

<Patch id="time/slew" show={["glide", "fall", "type", "unit"]} />

<Try>

Press Play and walk up the keyboard with **Rise** at about a fifth of a second.
Each note slides into the next. Take **Rise** to zero and it is an ordinary
keyboard again — the module is still there, doing nothing, which is what a
portamento switch really is.

</Try>

<Try>

Set **Rise** long and **Fall** short and watch the scope while you play a
repeated two-note figure. Slow up, fast down: that asymmetry is the shark's
tooth, and it is unreachable with any single glide knob.

</Try>

<Try>

Turn **Glide in hertz** on and play the same run. Low down it crawls; high up it
snaps. The interval your ear hears is a ratio, and a fixed number of hertz per
second is a smaller and smaller ratio the higher the note.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

This is why the unit matters more than the module. Pitch is logarithmic: an
octave is a doubling wherever you are, so an even glide has to be even in
**cents**, and the oscillator's detune input takes cents for exactly that
reason. A voltage-controlled oscillator with a linear frequency input glides the
lopsided way, which is audible on plenty of vintage instruments and is
occasionally the sound people are after. The other toggle asks a separate
question: <Term>exponential</Term> slew approaches its target the way a
capacitor charges, <Term>linear</Term> slew at a constant rate. That is the
shape of the approach, not the units it is measured in.

</Aside>
