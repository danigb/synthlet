The one thing to take from Part 35 is that a drum head's 0,1 mode produces
**two** frequencies, at roughly a hundred and eighty and three hundred and
thirty hertz. The ratio is 1.83. It is not an octave, and a snare built from an
octave pair is a snare that sounds like a synthesiser. The TR-909 used exactly
those two, each with a VCA and a contour of its own — and Reid notes that his
own model does not, because the 0,1 partials decay far faster than the rest,
sometimes at more than twice the rate.

<Try>

Hit it and watch the two marks. **Tone** moves them together and the ratio
between them never changes, because the upper one is derived from the lower.
The upper mode dies in less than half the time of the lower, which is what the
909 did and what a drum does.

</Try>

<Try>

Walk **Harder** from zero to one and hit it at each end. One knob is moving two
things: the noise level and the band-pass corner. That is Reid's pair of
generalisations in one gesture — a harder strike puts more energy at high
frequencies, and the more the snares interact with the head, the wider the
modes become, eventually changing into a complex noise spectrum. At the top the
drum has stopped being a drum with a rattle on it.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch **Drum** to **Handclap** and hit it. The same noise generator, band-passed
and then chopped by a ramp running at a hundred hertz, which is what turns one
burst into the several claps of a pair of hands.

</Try>

<Aside kind="history">

There is no handclap anywhere in Synth Secrets. Parts 35 and 36 never mention
one, and Part 41 closes by listing hand claps among the sounds Reid never got
round to. The circuit here is the TR-808's, and that is who to credit for it —
this page is the one place in the chapter where the recipe has no citation at
all, and it seemed more honest to say so than to quietly attach the nearest
article number.

</Aside>
