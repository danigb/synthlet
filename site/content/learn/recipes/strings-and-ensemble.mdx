Reid builds a string section out of beating. A hundred hertz against a hundred
and one beats once a second, and a section is a great many of those at once.
Two oscillators a few cents apart is the cheap version, and it is most of the
way there.

Then he points out the thing that makes a string machine sound bigger than its
oscillator count. A pulse-width-modulated wave from a **single** oscillator
already contains two pitches — one fixed and one swinging around it — so two
detuned PWM oscillators are four voices, not two.

<Try>

Hold a chord with **Fine detune** at zero, then walk it out to twenty cents. At
zero it is a synthesiser. Somewhere around eight it becomes a section, and past
about thirty it becomes out of tune. The beat rate follows the pitch, which is
why the effect stays the same size up the keyboard.

</Try>

<Try>

Put the detune back and raise **PWM depth** instead. Nothing about the tuning
has changed and the sound thickens anyway: that is the second pitch inside the
one oscillator, swinging.

</Try>

<Try>

Turn **Chorus mix** to zero and back. Then walk **Chorus** through its three
voicings while a chord is held. The Juno is two delay lines, the Ensemble is
three, and the Dimension is the difference output, which is wide without any
vibrato in it.

</Try>

<Aside kind="history">

The Solina String Ensemble's cabinet is in Part 62, not in the string articles
at all. It is three delay lines modulated a hundred and twenty degrees apart by
a pair of LFOs, one at about one hertz and the other at about six, with delay
times between ten and fifty milliseconds. The **Ensemble** voicing here is that
circuit. It is the reason a string machine from 1974 sounds like a string
machine and a pair of detuned oscillators does not.

</Aside>
