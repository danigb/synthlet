A cymbal is the hardest thing in the chapter to measure. James Boyk put a crash
cymbal in front of an instrument that reaches a hundred kilohertz and found no
sign of it running out of energy there, with about forty percent of its energy
above twenty kilohertz — above, that is, anything you can hear or record.

Part 39 describes what Roland did instead. Six square waves, tuned
enharmonically, split by filters into a low band and an upper band that is
split again, each with its own amplifier and attack-decay contour, the highest
band decaying fastest, then high-passed and recombined by a single Tone
control.

<Try>

Hit it and look at the six marks. No two of them are a whole-number ratio
apart, which is what "enharmonic" means and why the result has no pitch. Reid
never gives these six frequencies — they are the library's choice, taken from
its own drum machine, and only the shape of the recipe is his.

</Try>

<Try>

Pull **Low band** to zero and shorten the **Decay**, then switch **Mode** to
**Hi-hat** and compare. They are close, because Part 38's own point is that a
hi-hat is a cymbal with the bands moved and the decays shortened. It is a
setting, not a second instrument.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **FM ping** and hit it. A pulse at about a kilohertz modulating a
square at about two and a half, driven into both linear and logarithmic FM at
maximum, through a band-pass swept down to a kilohertz over a fifth of a second
for the ping and a high-pass at 2.64 kilohertz for the tail, with the tail
mixed louder than the ping. It contains no noise generator whatsoever.

</Try>

<Aside kind="history">

That last patch is not from a drum machine. Reid built it on a Nord Micro
Modular, and every frequency in it is his: 2.5 kHz, 1 kHz, the 1 kHz band-pass
landing, the 2.64 kHz high-pass, the two hundred millisecond opening and the
3.7 second close. It is the one cymbal in the chapter where the numbers are
citations and the only thing this patch chose was how loud to make it.

</Aside>
