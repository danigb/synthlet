Reid spends three articles on brass and gets the patch out of the way in one
paragraph: a sawtooth at four foot on a Minimoog, mixer at half, loudness
attack around a hundred milliseconds, sustain full, cutoff low, amount of
contour about two thirds, a little resonance. What the other two articles are
about is why that is not enough.

<Try>

Play a note and listen to the first fifty milliseconds. **Filter envelope** is
the amount of contour: the tone opens after the note has already started, which
is the single feature that says brass. Turn it to zero and you have an organ
with a slow attack. Reid measured a trumpet at 256 Hz taking about a dozen
cycles — fifty milliseconds — to settle into its waveform, and that is the
window the envelope is working in.

</Try>

<Try>

Raise **Key tracking** and play two octaves. A real trumpet is not a fixed
filter on a variable oscillator, so the cutoff has to follow the pitch or the
top of the range goes dull.

</Try>

<Try>

Set **Vibrato delay** to about a second and hold a long note. The vibrato is
not there at the start and arrives while you hold, the way a player's does, and
Reid's warning about it is that the amplitude must be very low: too much and
the timbre stops sounding like an instrument at all.

</Try>

<Aside kind="history">

The growl at the front of a brass note is a triangle wave at about eighty hertz
on the cutoff, gated by its own short contour. Eighty hertz is not a vibrato
speed — it is an audio frequency — and Reid raises it to make a point about
instruments rather than about brass: an ARP Axxe cannot make this sound, because
its LFO stops at twenty. Here the modulation rate stops at forty for the same
kind of reason, deliberately. Push **LFO rate** to the top and you are standing
at the wall Reid is describing. What is on the other side of it is not a faster
LFO, it is frequency modulation, which chapter 5 has its own page for.

</Aside>
