Part 41 makes a claim you can falsify in about four seconds. The Roland
CR-8000's cowbell is two tones, at five hundred and eighty-seven hertz and
eight hundred and forty-five — a ratio of 1 to 1.44 — taken by ear off the
machine, and even small deviations from these pitches, he writes, destroy the
cowbell illusion.

They are triangle waves rather than pulses, because pulses are far too bright
and synth-y, through a twelve decibel per octave band-pass at 2.64 kilohertz
with a little resonance, on a two-stage contour: a high-amplitude,
short-duration impact followed by a more extended tail.

<Try>

Hit it, then drag **Upper tone** from 845 up to 900 and hit it again. Fifty-five
hertz is nothing — half a semitone at that pitch — and the cowbell is gone. Put
it back and it returns.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **Claves** and hit it. One mode, no filter, no envelope: Part 41 says
the TR-808's claves are a single bridged-T oscillator and an output amplifier,
and that the signal is not even modified before being passed to the output.
Reid gives no frequency for them at all, only that the decay is shorter than
the cowbell's, so the pitch here is the library's.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **Bell — struck** and lengthen the **Decay**. The strike partials sit
at two, three and four times the pitch you hear, so the ear supplies the
missing one: Reid's example is a bell with partials at 100, 150 and 200 hertz
that you hear as fifty.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **Bell — ring modulation** and move **Bell carrier** and **Bell
modulator**. Two sines multiplied together give you the sum and the difference
and neither of the originals, which is Part 11's third case, and it is every
cheap bell in every cheap synthesiser.

</Try>

<Aside kind="note">

The spectrum is drawn linearly to half the sample rate, so at the cowbell's
setting the two tones are about six pixels apart. Watch the two marks rather
than the picture: the marks are where the book says the energy should be, and
the trace is where it is.

</Aside>
