Part 33 builds the obvious kick drum: a sine wave, one envelope on its
loudness, another on its pitch, and a click at the front. The number worth
having is the pitch depth. Reid measured a typical kick drum shifting by **a
couple of semitones** from start to finish, not by an octave — the loudness
changes by a hundred percent while the pitch changes by about ten — which is
why the pitch CV needs an attenuator and most drum machines sound wrong without
one.

Part 34 then builds the same drum on an ARP Axxe that has no oscillator left to
spare: resonance at maximum, cutoff at minimum, contour to filter about half,
attack zero, decay and release about half, no keyboard tracking, transposed two
octaves down. The filter is the oscillator, and the trigger is the beater.

<Try>

Hit it on **Route** zero and watch the contour. The pitch and the loudness fall
together over the same time, which is the whole drum. Lengthen the **Decay**
and both get longer, because both envelopes read the same number.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **Self-oscillating ladder** and hit it again. There is no oscillator
in that route: one impulse goes into a filter wound up to just under the point
where it sings, and what comes out is a drum. Reid backs the resonance off a
tad on purpose, so that a little of the excitation gets through.

</Try>

<Try>

Push **Drive** to the top on route zero. The saturator is what turns a thud
into a wall, and it is doing something a volume control cannot: the waveform
changes shape rather than size.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Turn **Click** to zero and hit it again. There is still a click. With an attack
of zero the amplifier's own discontinuity is one — the sound jumps from nothing
to full in a single sample — and Reid calls that the desirable consequence of
truly snappy contour generators and VCAs. The click generator is there to make
it louder, not to create it.

</Aside>

<Aside kind="history">

The TR-808's bridged-T oscillator goes slightly flat at long decay settings,
which is exactly what a kick drum does. The CR-68 and CR-78 before it did not,
and Reid names that as one reason analogue drum machines started sounding so
much better from the 808 onwards.

</Aside>
