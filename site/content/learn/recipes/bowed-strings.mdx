Everyone synthesises strings from a sawtooth, and most people think of it as a
convenient approximation. Part 48 shows that it is not one. A bowed string
sticks to the bow, is dragged, lets go, flies back and is caught again, and the
force that this stick-slip cycle exerts **at the bridge** — which is what the
body of the instrument hears — is a sawtooth. The saw is the measurement.

Part 49 then gives the body as a table, off an Analogue Systems RS360 filter
bank: three resonances at three hundred hertz, seven hundred hertz and three
kilohertz, with Q values of 3.5, 3.5 and 2. Those six numbers are Reid's.

<Try>

Pull all three formant sliders down to zero and play. What is left is a bare
sawtooth through an envelope: recognisably a synthesiser and recognisably not
an instrument. Bring them back one at a time and watch the marks. The body is
the whole difference.

</Try>

<Try>

Play up two octaves with the formants in place. The three marks do not move.
A body resonance is a property of the wooden box and not of the note, which is
the opposite of the key-tracking filter chapter 4 built.

</Try>

<Try>

Shorten the **Attack** to its minimum and play. A bow cannot start a note
instantly — it has to get the string moving — and an attack of a few
milliseconds turns a violin into a synth brass patch.

</Try>

<Try>

Set **Vibrato depth** to about twenty cents and **Vibrato delay** to a second
and a half. Reid's range is five to eight hertz and up to about a quarter tone,
arriving after the initial bowing rather than on it.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

The three filters here are in series rather than in parallel, and Part 50 is the
licence for that: his own Sorceror patch puts a resonant high-pass and a fixed
filter bank one after the other, so that the responses multiply rather than add.
A body resonance boosts a region of whatever is driving it. It does not replace
the driver with a filtered copy of itself.

</Aside>
