Part 54 italicises the sentence the whole recipe turns on: a flute does not get
significantly louder or softer as the player alters the blowing pressure, it
becomes brighter or duller. So the patch has no tremolo, no vibrato, and one
modulator, and that modulator goes to the filter.

The rest is two fixed corners. The low-pass sits at two kilohertz whatever note
is played — that number is Reid's, off an ARP Axxe patch, with only a few
percent of pitch tracking. The high-pass is the one figure he never gives, and
says only that it is "in the region of a few hundred Hertz", so the three
hundred here is this patch's choice rather than his.

<Try>

Play and move **Brightness modulation**. The two marks on the spectrum are the
fixed corners; the trace under the upper one moves and the mark does not.
Trained players vary their blowing pressure by about ten percent at five to six
hertz, which is what this is.

</Try>

<Try>

Turn **Chiff** down to zero and play a phrase. The puff of air at the front of
the note is, in Reid's words, perhaps the most defining characteristic of the
instrument, and without it the patch is a filtered sawtooth.

</Try>

<Try>

Shorten the **Release** and play a legato line. Reid calls the result a
"sucking" between the notes, and lengthening the release is his own fix for it.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch on **Pan pipe**. A pipe closed at the bottom has no even harmonics at
all, so this is not the flute with a filter on it: it is six tuned resonances,
odd ones only, excited by noise. Reid's practical answer for it was six
band-pass filters on the edge of self-oscillation.

</Try>

<Aside kind="note">

**Breath** starts at zero on purpose. Reid recommends against putting noise in
a flute patch — he calls it a mistake that detracts from the authenticity
rather than adding to it. The knob is here so you can disagree with him, and
now you know that you are.

</Aside>
