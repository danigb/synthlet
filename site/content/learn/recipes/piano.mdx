Reid takes a Roland JX10 patch called Piano 1-B apart and finds one trick doing
all the work. The first oscillator is an eight foot square and is the master.
The second is a four foot sawtooth, hard-synced to it, tuned up by two
semitones and ten cents — so it sits fourteen semitones and ten cents above the
master — with the envelope depth at ninety-nine. At note-on that envelope
throws the slave up and drops it back through the sync in a quarter of a
second, and the resulting blip is the hammer strike.

The master is audible too, at about a quarter of the mix, which is why the note
changes character as it goes on rather than simply getting quieter.

<Try>

Set **Layer balance** to zero so you hear only the thunk, then take **Sync
sweep** to zero and play. Without the sweep the layer is a synced pair sitting
still, which is a fixed buzz. The whole hammer is in that one envelope.

</Try>

<Try>

Shorten **Sweep decay** to its minimum and lengthen it to its maximum. Too
short and the strike is a click; too long and the piano turns into a sync lead,
which is what the same patch is if you leave the envelope running.

</Try>

<Try>

Walk **Layer balance** from zero to one while playing. Neither end is a piano.
Reid's rule for why layering works is that the two sounds have to be similar
enough to be indistinguishable within the composite and different enough to
make something more interesting than either, and this is that sentence as a
slider.

</Try>

<Aside kind="history">

Part 45 ends with the most useful sentence in the chapter. Even when carefully
programmed on a large modular synthesiser, Reid writes, the result sounds like
a Wurlitzer or a Hohner Pianet. It does not sound like a Bosendorfer. The
reason is in the previous lesson's stiffness knob: a piano string's partials
are stretched, every note has three strings slightly out of tune with each
other, and the whole frame resonates. Two oscillators and a sync cannot get
there, and knowing exactly which corner cannot be turned is worth more than
another hour of tweaking.

</Aside>
