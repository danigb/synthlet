Part 28 works out what plucking a string actually does. Pull it at the centre
and let go and it starts as a triangle, which has odd harmonics only, falling
as one over n squared. Pull it a third of the way along and every third
harmonic is missing; a quarter of the way and every fourth. Slide the picking
position and Reid says you hear a swept comb filter — because that is exactly
what it is.

Then Part 30 ends with a verdict. You cannot, he writes, create
authentic-sounding acoustic guitar patches using analogue subtractive
synthesis. He is right, and this page is here because the module below is not
subtractive synthesis: it is a delay line with a loss in it, which is what a
string physically is.

<Try>

Play a note and drag **Pick position** slowly. The marks on the spectrum are
the comb's first three notches, and they move with the knob. Put it on 0.25 and
the first notch stands on the fourth harmonic, which is Reid's own arithmetic
arriving from the other direction.

</Try>

<Try>

Set the position to zero. The comb is bypassed entirely — that is the bridge,
where there is nothing to cancel — and the tone goes bright and characterless.

</Try>

<Try>

Raise **Stiffness** and play low notes. The partials stop being whole-number
multiples: a real string resists bending, so the high ones travel faster and
sit sharp. That inharmonicity is most of what separates a piano or a clavinet
from a comb, and it is the next lesson's problem too.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Reid's conclusion was about his instrument, not about physics. A subtractive
synthesiser starts with a waveform and takes things away, and a plucked string
is not a waveform with things taken away: it is energy going round a loop and
losing a little each time, faster at the top than at the bottom. Once the loop
is the module, the picking position and the pick angle and the stiffness all
stop being approximations and start being the actual parameters.

</Aside>
