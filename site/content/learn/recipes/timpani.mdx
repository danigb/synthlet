Part 31 works out what a circular drum head does on its own: modes at 1, 1.59,
2.14, 2.3 and so on, ratios that fall where a Bessel function crosses zero and
sound like nothing in particular. Part 32 then explains why a kettle drum has a
pitch anyway. The shell traps air under the skin, the air loads the radial
modes, and four of them get dragged into a near-harmonic series at 1, 1.5, 1.98
and 2.44 — a hundred and fifty, two hundred and twenty-five, two hundred and
ninety-seven and three hundred and sixty-six hertz, in his table.

<Try>

Strike it and look at the four marks. They are Reid's Table 1 ratios, drawn at
whatever **Principal** is set to, and off centre they stand on four peaks.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch **Strike** to **Dead centre** and hit it again. The marks now stand on a
mess, because the mode table underneath has been swapped for the ideal
membrane's twelve Bessel ratios. Reid describes striking a kettle drum in the
middle as producing a dull, toneless and musically uninteresting thump, and a
timpanist strikes almost precisely a quarter of the way from the edge to the
centre in order not to.

</Try>

<Try>

Set a long **Decay** and listen to the end of the note. Mode one rings for
forty-five percent of the decay time and mode three for ninety-one, so as it
dies the lowest mode goes first and the pitch you hear can leap a fifth or an
octave on the way out.

</Try>

<Aside kind="note">

The principal is the 1,1 mode and not the fundamental. The true fundamental of
the head sits at about sixty-three percent of it and is barely present, which
is the second reason the pitch is unstable at the end of a roll. On the
spectrum it would be at about ninety-five hertz, which on a linear axis to half
the sample rate is two pixels from the left edge.

</Aside>
