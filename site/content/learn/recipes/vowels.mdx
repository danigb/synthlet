Part 23 prints a table of the first three formants of six adult male vowels,
and it is the most immediately useful table in the series: ee at 270, 2300 and
3000 hertz, oo at 300, 870 and 2250, i at 400, 2000 and 2550, e at 530, 1850
and 2500, u at 640, 1200 and 2400, and a at 660, 1700 and 2400. Three formants
are enough to be understood — experiments in the 1950s established that — and
with six, Reid says, the results can be very lifelike indeed.

<Patch id="recipes/vowels" show={["vowel", "source", "morph"]} />

<Try>

Play and walk **Vowel** down the list. The three marks jump each time, and the
middle one moves furthest between most pairs. That is Reid's point about what
the ear is actually listening to: the second formant is the one that carries
the vowel.

</Try>

<Try>

Hold a key and drag **Morph** slowly. It interpolates from the vowel you chose
to the next one down the list and wraps round at the end, and it does it
geometrically, so what you hear is a mouth opening rather than two sounds
crossfading.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch **Source** to **Noise** and play anything. The vowel is still there and
the pitch is gone, because a whisper has no pitch and still says the word. Then
play up two octaves with the sawtooth back: the marks do not move at all.
Formants are a property of the mouth and not of the note, which is exactly what
a key-tracking filter is not.

</Try>

<Aside kind="note">

Two small honesties. Reid's extended table gives the third formant a Q of
fifty, and the filter here stops at forty, so that one is clamped and the third
resonance is a little broader than he asked for. And the spectrum below is
linear to half the sample rate, which puts the first formant of **ee** at 270
hertz about six pixels from the left edge — so for this lesson the marks are
the evidence and the trace is the confirmation.

</Aside>
