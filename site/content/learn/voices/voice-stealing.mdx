Every polyphonic instrument has a fixed number of voices, and the interesting
question is not how many. It is what happens on the note after the last one.
Part 21 is the first place most people meet the word **stealing**, and the
answer it describes is the one nearly everything ships: take the voice that has
been sounding longest, because it is the note the listener is least attached to.

That is not the only defensible answer, and the release time is what makes the
argument audible. With a long tail, four notes are still using their voices well
after your fingers left them.

<Patch id="voices/stealing" show={["voices", "steal", "release"]} />

<Try>

Press Play with **Voices** at four and **Release** long. Hold a low interval with
your left hand and play a rising line above it. On **Protect** the two held notes
survive everything: the line simply stops sounding once the pool is full, which
is startling the first time and is exactly what you want from a bass pedal. On
**LRU** the oldest goes first, and your held interval is the first casualty.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **MRU** and play the same line. Now the newest note is the one taken,
which comes out as a stutter — each new note kills the one before it. It sounds
like a mistake and it is occasionally precisely the effect somebody is after.

</Try>

<Try>

Drop **Voices** to two and play a chord. Everything below the limit is a
different instrument, and a sustain-heavy sound at two voices is mostly a
sequence of interruptions.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

A pool is chosen when it is built. The voice count and the stealing rule are
read once, at construction, and there is no setter for either — so moving
**Voices** here builds a second instrument and carries the sound across as a
preset. A note in flight stops when that happens, and that is not a limitation
being hidden: reallocating a pool under a held chord is the alternative, and it
is worse.

</Aside>
