A monophonic synthesiser has one voice and a keyboard with sixty-one keys, so
somebody had to decide what happens when two are down at once. Part 18 finds
four answers in the instruments of the period, and none of them is a compromise:
they are four different things to play.

**Lowest note** is the Minimoog's, and it is the one that gives you the drone
trick — hold a bass note, play a melody above it, and the moment you let go of
the melody the bass comes back, because it was always the lowest. **Highest
note** is the Korg 700's and it does the same thing upside down, which suits a
lead line over a held pedal. **Last note** is the ARP's, and it is the one that
always speaks: whatever you just pressed is what you hear.

<Try>

Press Play, then hold a low note and add three above it one at a time without
releasing any. On **Lowest** nothing changes after the first key. On **Highest**
the pitch climbs with every addition. On **Last** it follows your fingers
exactly. The contour scope is the easiest way to see what happened.

</Try>

<Try>

Now turn **Legato** on and play a run without lifting your finger between notes.
The pitch moves but the attack does not happen again — one envelope for the
whole phrase. Turn it off and every note is born from silence. That single
switch is most of what people mean when they describe a Minimoog line as smooth.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Neither of these is a setting on the instrument here; both are part of the
**preset**. A bass sound is its note priority in the way a lead sound is its
glide, so they travel with the sound rather than sitting beside it, and loading
a patch loads them. The code panel shows what that costs: reading the current
preset, changing one key, and writing it back.

</Aside>
