Polyphony costs money. A voice is an oscillator, a filter, two envelope
generators and an amplifier, and in 1978 that was a circuit board — so several
famous instruments bought the oscillators and shared everything after them. Four
oscillators, one filter, one envelope: a **paraphonic** synthesiser, which plays
chords and is not a four-voice polysynth.

The widget below is both, built from the same parts with the same numbers, so
the only difference between the two arms is where the filter and the envelope
sit.

<Patch id="voices/paraphonic" show={["mode", "attack", "release"]} />

<Try>

Press Play with **Architecture** on **Paraphonic** and **Attack** at about a
fifth of a second. Hold one note, wait for it to arrive, then add a second
without releasing the first. The new note does not fade in — it appears at full
level, because the shared envelope is already open and there is nothing left for
it to do.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to **Polyphonic** and do it again. Now each note fades in on its own,
because each has an envelope of its own. Then release one finger of a held
chord: on Polyphonic that note decays and the others continue; on Paraphonic
nothing happens until the last finger leaves, and then everything stops
together.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

This is exactly the sound of a string machine, and it is why the Polymoog and
the Korg Poly-800 are described that way rather than as polysynths. The shared
filter has a second consequence worth listening for: a filter sweep moves every
note in the chord at once, which is lush and is also why a paraphonic instrument
cannot make one note in a chord brighter than another. Everything after the
oscillators is common.

</Aside>

The polyphonic arm here is hand-rolled — four copies of the same three modules —
so the comparison stays about architecture. A real instrument builds that pool
once and manages it, which is what the next page is about.
