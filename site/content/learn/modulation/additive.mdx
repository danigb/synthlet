Chapter 1 took a sawtooth apart into harmonics. This page builds one back up by
hand. Nine oscillators at one, two, three… nine times 220 Hz, nine amplifiers,
one mixer: Reid calls that configuration hopelessly inefficient in the same
paragraph in which he points out that a Hammond organ is exactly it, with ninety
years of tonewheels standing in for the oscillators.

<Try>

Press Play, then **1/n**. The nth slider lands at roughly one nth of the first
and the sound is a sawtooth — thin, because nine harmonics is where the series
has been cut, but unmistakably the waveform chapter 1 started from. Press **All
equal** and every partial jumps to full: much brighter, and unlike anything you
would find in nature, because real resonators do not hand out energy evenly.

</Try>

<Try>

Press **88 8000 000**. Three drawbars out, the first three harmonics, and the
result is instantly an organ — the registration Jimmy Smith and Keith Emerson
played on. Then pull the sliders about by hand. There is no filter on this
widget and no waveform list; every timbre you can reach is reached by deciding
how loud each sine wave is, which is the whole of additive synthesis.

</Try>

<Try>

Press **Pluck** and hold your eye on the spectrum. Every partial starts falling
at once, but the ninth reaches silence in a ninth of the time the first takes,
so the sound darkens as it decays. Nothing filtered it. Set **Decay** long and
short and listen again: a plucked string, from nine straight lines.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

This is the one patch in the tutorial with no worklet in it — nine
`OscillatorNode`s and a handful of `GainNode`s, nothing else. That is
deliberate. A native
node is a synthlet module too, and additive synthesis is the case that proves it,
because it needs no filter, no envelope generator and no processor of any kind:
the amplitude of each harmonic over time _is_ the sound, so the contours the
partials trace out do the work that a filter and a VCA would otherwise be hired
for.

</Aside>
