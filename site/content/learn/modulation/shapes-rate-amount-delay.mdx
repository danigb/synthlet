The last page hid the LFO's own controls and asked only where its output went.
This is the same synth with the switch left alone — it is still on pitch — and
the oscillator opened up instead. An LFO is an oscillator, so it has a waveform
and a frequency. Because it is a modulator rather than a voice it has two things
a voice has no use for.

<Try>

Hold a key and walk down the **Shape** list with **Depth** high. Sine is a
singer's vibrato. Square is a trill: two pitches and nothing in between. The two
ramps are sirens, one rising and one falling, and they are not interchangeable —
your ear notices which way a pitch is travelling. Random steps to a new value
each cycle; smooth random glides to it instead.

</Try>

<Try>

Put the shape back on sine and take **Rate** up. Past about 12 Hz you stop
counting cycles and start hearing one wobbling thing; past 20 Hz the wobble
disappears entirely and something else arrives in its place. Everything from
lesson five onwards lives on the far side of that boundary.

</Try>

<Try>

Set **Delay** to one second and **Fade-in** to one and a half, then hold a key
for three. The note starts perfectly straight, waits, and then grows its vibrato.
Let go and press again: the delay restarts with the note. That is how a flute
player phrases, and a fixed vibrato from the first sample is the single loudest
tell of an unplayed synthesiser.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Delay is not a convenience knob, it is a second envelope. Reid meets it on the
Korg MS20, whose simpler contour generator is a DAR — delay, attack, release —
put there precisely so that the vibrato it drives can arrive late. What this
patch calls **Delay** and **Fade-in** are that envelope's first two stages,
gated by the key you are holding, and the LFO's depth is what they multiply.
A modulator with an envelope on it is the ordinary case, not the exotic one.

</Aside>
