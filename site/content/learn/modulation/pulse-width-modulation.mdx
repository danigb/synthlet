A square wave spends half of each cycle at the top and half at the bottom. Let it
spend a third up and two thirds down and it is still a pulse wave, but a
different-sounding one — and Reid gives the law behind that in a single sentence:
a pulse whose <Term>duty cycle</Term> is one in three has a sawtooth's harmonics
with every third one missing, one in four with every fourth one missing, and so
on for any whole number you like. The marks on the spectrum below are the harmonic
series of the note. Which of them has a peak standing on it is what the width
decides.

<Patch id="modulation/pwm" show={["width", "depth", "rate"]} />

<Try>

Put **PWM depth** at zero and drag **Width** slowly from 0.5 downwards. At 0.5
every second mark is bare — that is the square wave, odd harmonics only. Near
0.33 the gaps move to every third mark; near 0.25, to every fourth. Between those
values nothing vanishes completely, it is merely thinned, and the sound thickens
from hollow towards nasal as you narrow it.

</Try>

<Try>

Leave **Width** at 0.5, bring **PWM depth** up and set **PWM rate** to about half
a hertz. Now the gaps travel: a slow shimmer that never repeats the same spectrum
twice. This is the sound of every string machine of the seventies, and it is one
oscillator — no chorus pedal, no second voice, no detuning.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **PWM rate** up to 6 or 7 Hz. It stops shimmering and starts buzzing, because
a spectrum changing thirty times a second is no longer something you hear as
movement.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

The moving harmonics are the visible half of it. The audible half is stranger:
a pulse whose width is modulated by a triangle carries _two_ pitches at once, the
oscillator's own and a second one bending above and below it at the modulation
rate. That is why PWM sounds chorused rather than merely animated, and why Reid
reaches for it when he wants an ensemble out of a synth that has one oscillator
and no effects at all.

</Aside>
