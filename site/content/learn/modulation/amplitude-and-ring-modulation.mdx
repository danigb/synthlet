Take the tremolo from the first page of this chapter and speed the modulator up
until it is a note in its own right. You might expect very fast tremolo. What you
get instead are frequencies that were in neither signal: a <Term>sum</Term> and
a <Term>difference</Term>, at carrier plus modulator and carrier minus
modulator. The marks on the spectrum are those two predictions, with the carrier
between them.

<Patch id="modulation/ring" show={["carrier", "modulator", "offset"]} />

<Try>

Press Play with the defaults — carrier 2000 Hz, modulator 1000 Hz, **Ring →
amplitude** at zero. Peaks stand on the outer two marks, at 1000 and 3000 Hz, and
the middle mark is bare. The modulator is not there either. A ring modulator
outputs the sum and the difference and neither of its inputs.

</Try>

<Try>

Drag **Ring → amplitude** up to 1. The carrier rises back into the middle mark
and the sidebands drop to half their height, which is amplitude modulation rather
than ring modulation — the difference between the two is a DC offset on the
modulator, and on an ARP 2600 it was a switch.

</Try>

<Try>

Leave the modulator at 1000 Hz and take the carrier up to about 3700. The
sidebands follow the marks out to roughly 2700 and 4700, which are in no simple
ratio to anything, and the sound goes metallic. This is Reid's third case at ten
times his frequencies: a ring modulator is enharmonic almost
everywhere, which is why it makes bells and Daleks and almost never makes notes.

</Try>

<Aside kind="note">

Reid's own numbers are 200 Hz and 100 Hz. These are ten times his, because the
spectrum here runs linearly to 22 kHz and three peaks 100 Hz apart would be three
pixels apart. The arithmetic is about ratios, so nothing is lost but the absolute
values.

</Aside>

<Aside kind="why">

It is a line of school trigonometry. The product of two cosines is half the
cosine of their sum plus half the cosine of their difference — so multiplying two
sine waves does not produce a modulated sine wave at all, it produces exactly two
new sine waves and nothing else. Every extra partial in either input multiplies
against every partial in the other, which is why feeding two sawtooths into a
ring modulator produces a spectrum nobody can predict by ear.

</Aside>
