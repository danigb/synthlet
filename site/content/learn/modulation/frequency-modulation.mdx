Amplitude modulation at audio rate gave two new frequencies. Frequency modulation
gives an unlimited family of them, at the carrier plus or minus every whole
multiple of the modulator: c ± m, c ± 2m, c ± 3m, and onwards. The marks below are
that prediction. The carrier is fixed at 1 kHz, so the only two things you can
change are where the modulator sits relative to it and how hard it pushes.

<Patch id="modulation/fm" show={["ratio", "index", "rate"]} />

<Try>

Take **Modulator** down to about five hertz and put **Index** at about 5, which
is a deviation of twenty-five hertz either side of the carrier. That is vibrato —
you can count the wobble. Now drag the slider
upwards. At twenty or thirty hertz the wobble smears; by a few hundred it has
stopped being movement and become tone colour, and by 1 kHz the spectrum has
filled with sidebands standing on the marks. Nothing changed but one slider.

</Try>

<Try>

Put **Index** at 1 and step down the **C:M ratio** list. At 1:1 the sidebands land
on every harmonic of the carrier and the result sounds like a filtered sawtooth.
At 1:2 only the odd harmonics survive, which is a square. At 1:3 every third is
gone, which is a narrow pulse. Three familiar waveforms, from two sine waves.

</Try>

<Try>

Choose 1:1.21 and listen, then watch. No peak sits on a harmonic of anything; the
partials are packed at uneven spacings and the sound is a struck bell rather than
a note. Now raise **Index** from 0 to 20 on any ratio: at zero there is only the
carrier, and every step upwards buys more sidebands and pushes energy out of the
middle into them.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

At 1:1 the lower sidebands run 0, −1000, −2000 Hz and so on, which looks like
nonsense until you accept Reid's answer: a negative frequency is the same
frequency with its phase inverted. It reflects off zero and is subtracted from
whatever is already there. That is why the mark list here takes an absolute
value, and why a 1:1 ratio draws a plain harmonic series instead of cancelling
itself to silence.

</Aside>

Stacking operators, and feeding a modulator back into itself, are the other half
of FM; both need a module this library does not have yet, so this page stops at
two oscillators and the C:M ratio.
