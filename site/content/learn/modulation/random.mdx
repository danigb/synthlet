Every modulator so far has repeated. A <Term>sample and hold</Term> does not, and
the circuit behind it is two components: a switch and a capacitor. Close the
switch for an instant and the capacitor charges to whatever voltage is at the
input; open it again and that voltage has nowhere to go, so it stays. Feed the
input from a noise generator, close the switch on a clock, and the output is a
staircase of random values — the first picture below, which is a control voltage
and not a sound.

<Try>

Press Play and watch the staircase while you listen. Each step is one closing of
the switch. Take **Clock** from 300 up towards 900 beats per minute and the steps
narrow; the burble speeds up and the trace redraws to match, because the picture
and the sound are the same voltage.

</Try>

<Try>

Pull **Depth** down to nothing. The steps are still there in the control voltage,
but the filter no longer moves and the saw sits still. Depth is how many hertz of
cutoff one unit of held voltage is worth — the amplifier that Reid draws in every
modulation path, here in its most obvious form.

</Try>

<Try>

Turn **Track and hold** on and widen **Pulse width** to 0.8. The steps grow
slopes: the circuit is now transparent while the clock pulse is high and only
latches on its falling edge, so four fifths of each beat is spent following the
noise rather than holding it. Narrow the pulse again and it is a sample and hold
once more.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Nothing here samples audio, and yet this is where digital sound begins. The same
two components in front of a converter, clocked fast enough, are the input stage
of every recording ever made — Reid's article walks from this burble to
sample-rate conversion in a few pages. The synth module is the slow, audible
version of the thing, running at five hertz instead of forty-four thousand, which
makes it a good place to learn what sampling actually does.

</Aside>
