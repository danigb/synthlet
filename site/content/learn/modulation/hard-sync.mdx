This last modulation does not arrive at a level or a cutoff. The master
oscillator's rising edge goes to the slave's <Term>sync</Term> input, and every
time it arrives the slave's waveform is thrown back to the start of its cycle. If
the slave is running faster than the master — and it usually is — it never gets
to finish. Only the slave is heard; the master is in this patch purely to
interrupt it.

<Try>

Press Play and drag **Slave** slowly from 660 Hz upwards with **Sweep** at zero.
The tone changes continuously and drastically — ramps, then strange hollow
variants, then ramps again — and the pitch does not move at all. Watch the left
mark on the spectrum: the lowest peak stays at the master's 220 Hz however far
the slave travels. That is the first rule of hard sync, and it is why a synth can
give you a timbre knob that plays in tune.

</Try>

<Try>

Now move **Master** instead. The pitch follows it, as it must, and the timbre
shifts with it too, because what matters is the ratio of the two. At 660 against
220 the slave completes exactly three cycles per reset and the output is a plain
ramp; a few hertz either side of that and it is anything but.

</Try>

<Try>

Put **Slave** back around 660, **Sweep** near 2000 and **Decay** at about half a
second, then press **Trigger**. The slave is flung upwards and falls back, so the
timbre sweeps down through all those variants in one gesture. That is the sound
a Moog Prodigy is remembered for, and it is one envelope on one oscillator's
pitch.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

The waveform repeats whenever the master resets it, so it repeats at the master's
period — and a wave that repeats at 220 Hz has its energy on harmonics of 220 Hz,
whatever shape each repetition happens to be. What the slave's frequency changes
is which of those harmonics are loud: it puts a broad peak in the spectrum around
itself and drags it about as you sweep. That is a formant under another name, and
it is why sync sounds vocal in a way that a filter sweep does not.

</Aside>
