A violinist rocks a finger on the string and the note wavers: the string gets a
little shorter, then a little longer, and the pitch follows. A synthesiser does
the same thing with an oscillator too slow to hear — a <Term>low-frequency
oscillator</Term>, an LFO — whose output is not a sound but a control voltage,
and which is therefore useless until you decide where to send it.

<Patch
  id="modulation/lfo-destinations"

/>

<Try>

Hold a key with **Destination** on **pitch**. The first picture is the LFO
itself, not the sound: one slow wave, five cycles a second. Bring **Depth** up
from nothing and the note starts to waver. That is vibrato. The cable arrives at
the oscillator's detune rather than its frequency, which is why the wobble is the
same size at the top of the keyboard as at the bottom — cents are a ratio, hertz
are not.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch **Destination** to **loudness**. Nothing changed but where the cable ends:
it now arrives at an amplifier's gain. The note swells and fades instead of
bending, and the effect has a different name. Tremolo and vibrato are confused
for each other constantly, and this is the whole of the difference.

</Try>

<Try>

Now **filter**, and sweep **Rate** across its travel. At 0.2 Hz you have an
ambient sweep. Around 1 or 2 Hz it is a wah. Push it to 15 Hz and the note
growls, which is half of every synthesised brass patch ever programmed.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

These are not three features that happen to resemble each other. Reid draws the
LFO once and then draws an amplifier after it per destination, each with its own
depth control, and that block diagram is this patch — which is also why a mod
wheel on a real synth can be routed to anything at all. A synthesiser is a small
number of modules and a large number of places to plug them into each other.
Modulation is the chapter where that stops being a slogan.

</Aside>
