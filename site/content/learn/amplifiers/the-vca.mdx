Imagine turning the volume knob on a hi-fi up and down twice a second while a
record plays. That is tremolo, and it is also the entire definition of
a <Term>voltage controlled amplifier</Term>: an amplifier whose gain is being
set by another signal instead of by a hand. Replace the hand with an oscillator
running below hearing and you have the patch below.

<Patch id="amplifiers/vca" show={["depth", "rate"]} />

<Try>

Press Play with **Depth** at zero. A steady tone, and a flat line on the
contour. Bring **Depth** up and the line starts to breathe: the LFO is being
added to the amplifier's own gain, so the level now swings either side of where
it was sitting.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **Depth** all the way to the top. The trough of each cycle now reaches
zero, so the sound switches off and on rather than swelling and fading. Tremolo
that deep stops being an effect on a note and becomes a rhythm.

</Try>

<Try>

Leave **Depth** halfway and run **Rate** up. Somewhere past ten cycles a second
your ear stops counting the pulses and starts hearing a roughness instead. At
the top of the slider the wobble is nearly a tone of its own, which is the
doorway into amplitude modulation and a later chapter.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

There is no module called VCA in this library and there does not need to be. A
gain node's gain is a parameter, a parameter accepts a connection, and a
connection carrying a signal is a control voltage — so the amplifier in the
diagram is a native browser node with a cable plugged into its knob.

Reid puts the general version of this at the end of Part 3 and calls it one of
the most important secrets in the series: in voltage terms there is no
difference at all between a signal you hear and a signal that shapes what you
hear. The oscillator above is a controller because of where its output goes, and
for no other reason. The next page takes the same idea from the other end.

</Aside>
