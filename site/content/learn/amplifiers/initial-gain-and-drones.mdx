The front panel of an ARP Odyssey has a slider marked Initial Gain, and the
first thing everybody does with it is turn it up and then spend a while working
out how to make the noise go away. All it does is add a steady voltage to
whatever the contour generator is sending the amplifier.

<Patch id="amplifiers/initial-gain" show={["initial"]} />

<Try>

With **Initial gain** at zero, play a few notes. Between them there is silence,
and the contour comes back down to the floor every time. The amplifier is doing
nothing at all unless the envelope tells it to.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **Initial gain** to about a fifth. There is now a drone under everything,
and it was there before you touched a key. The envelope still works — each note
still swells and falls — but it swells from the drone and falls back to it, and
the contour never returns to the bottom of the display.

</Try>

<Try>

Push it to the top. The notes are now a ripple on something much louder than
they are, which is the sound of an amplifier being told to stay open and only
occasionally being told a little more. Then bring the slider back to zero and
enjoy the silence you have earned.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

The circuit has no mixer in it, and it does not need one. A parameter adds up
everything connected to it, so the envelope's cable and the offset's cable meet
at the amplifier's gain and are summed there — which is precisely what a control
voltage mixer on a modular synth is, and the same trick the last lesson of the
envelope chapter used to draw a contour no ADSR could.

The musical use is the obvious one and the unobvious one at the same time. A
drone under a solo is a sound you cannot get from a keyboard alone, because a
keyboard only ever tells you about notes; the offset is the part of the patch
that is true whether or not anyone is playing.

</Aside>
