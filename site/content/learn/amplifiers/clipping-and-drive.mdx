Every amplifier so far has been assumed to have infinite headroom: ask for ten
times the signal and you get ten times the signal. Real ones run out. Past the
limit the tops of the waveform square off for as long as the demand is beyond
what the device has, and the sound turns harsh in a way that has nothing to do
with being loud.

The source here is a sine — one harmonic, nothing above it — so everything that
appears in the spectrum was put there by the amplifier.

<Patch id="amplifiers/clip" show={["drive", "output"]} />

<Try>

Press Play with **Drive** low. The waveform is a smooth curve and the spectrum
has a single peak, sitting on the first of the marks. Those marks are at the
odd multiples of the tone, bunched together at the left-hand end of the display,
well before the first gridline.

</Try>

<Try>

Run **Drive** up. The peaks of the wave flatten into plateaus, and one by one
the marks grow a spike under them: the third, then the fifth, then the seventh,
each weaker than the last. The tone gets a rasp on it that no filter put there,
because nothing has been taken away — something has been added.

</Try>

<Try>

Now make the comparison fair. Pull **Output** down as you push **Drive** up, so
that the two settings are about equally loud, and switch between them. What is
left when the loudness is matched is the distortion itself, which is the thing
worth learning to recognise.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

Only the odd harmonics appear, and the symmetry is why. The curve treats the top
of the wave exactly as it treats the bottom, so the output is still an odd
function of the input, and an odd function has no even harmonics to give. Break
the symmetry — clip one side harder than the other — and the even ones arrive,
which is roughly the difference between a fuzz pedal and a valve.

The curve here is a smooth one rather than a hard ceiling, so the corners are
rounded instead of cut square. That is soft clipping, and it is most of what
people mean when they call a circuit warm: the same harmonics, arriving
gradually as you play harder instead of all at once at a threshold.

</Aside>
