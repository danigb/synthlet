Reid's picture of a delay is a tape recorder with an infinitely long strip of
tape running past a record head and then a replay head. The distance between the
heads and the speed of the tape decide how late the copy is, and that is the
whole device. The knobs below are that picture and two things people added to
it.

<Patch id="effects/delay" show={["time", "feedback", "cross", "mix"]} />

<Try>

Press Play and listen to one pluck. With **Feedback** at zero you hear the
string and one copy of it, and **Time** is how far apart they are. Take **Mix**
to zero and the copy disappears; take it to one and the original does. Reid has
to draw that dry path as a separate cable, because the delay line itself only
ever produces the late copy.

</Try>

<Try>

Now raise **Feedback**. Some of the output is recorded again, so the copy makes
its own copy, and one repeat becomes a row of them getting quieter. Tape echoes
label this knob _Regeneration_, and on a real machine it is how much of the
signal the erase head leaves behind on each pass. Push it past one and the row
stops getting quieter: the machine sings, held in bounds by the soft limiter
inside the loop.

</Try>

<Try>

Put headphones on, set **Feedback** around 0.6, and bring **Cross-feed** up to
one. The repeats start alternating between the ears. The decay does not change
as you do it — the loop still loses exactly as much per trip, it just loses it
somewhere else each time.

</Try>

<Aside kind="history">

Ping-pong was esoteric in 1975 and is a preset now, but it began as a patch: two
delay lines with their feedback cables crossed over into each other's inputs.
Reid draws it with more mixers and multiples than seem necessary, and then
points out the compensation — with everything on separate cables you can give
the two channels different times, different feedbacks and different levels.

</Aside>
