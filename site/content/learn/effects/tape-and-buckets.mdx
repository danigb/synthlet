Reid asks a good question halfway through Part 60. A bucket-brigade device and a
digital delay are the same idea: store a slice of the signal, hold it, pass it
down a line of identical stages at a rate a clock decides. If the rate and the
number of stages match, why do they sound so different?

His answer is that in the bucket brigade every stage is a capacitor, every
capacitor is imperfect, and the errors accumulate. Nothing arrives at the far end
unaltered. Below, that is one knob.

<Try>

Press Play with **Feedback** around 0.55 and listen to the row of repeats, then
take **Age** from 0 to 1. Each generation loses more top end, gains more hiss
and saturates a little harder, until the fifth repeat is a dark blur of the
first. That is the accumulation, and it is the reason people buy old delays.

</Try>

<Try>

Drag **Time** slowly while the repeats are sounding. The pitch bends, the way it
does when you push a tape reel with your thumb, because this module moves the
heads rather than crossfading between two read positions. The digital delay on
the previous page does the opposite on purpose and holds the pitch steady.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch **Machine** from Tape to BBD at the same settings. Both read the same
line at several fixed head positions; the tape machine's are whole multiples of
the delay time, so extra heads arrive as a rhythm, while the bucket brigade's
are at awkward fractions and arrive as a wash.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

**Wobble** is separate from **Age** because a well-maintained machine with an
eccentric capstan is a real and desirable thing, and one composite wear knob
cannot say it. Age is everything getting old together; wobble is wow and flutter
on top of whatever age already implies.

</Aside>
