Reid ends Part 61 by building a reverb out of nothing but echo units. One delay
line repeating gives a stream of repeats. Feed that into a second one set to a
different time and every repeat grows its own stream. Feed that into a third and
the result, drawn, is the picture of natural reverberation he had published
three years earlier: a click, some early reflections, then a tail too dense to
count.

<Try>

Press Play on Reid's Figure 17 and turn **Decay** up. The repeats stop being
countable somewhere around the middle of the knob, which is the moment a
sequence of echoes becomes a space. Then listen to what is left ringing:
something metallic, on a pitch.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch **Model** to Greyhole and then to the plate while a pluck is decaying.
Both are made of delay lines too — they just have far more of them, arranged so
that each one smears the others. The metallic pitch goes away, because there is
no longer a small number of fixed times for it to come from.

</Try>

<Try>

Take **Mix** to zero and back. Two of these three models have no dry signal of
their own at all, so the knob here is a send and a return rather than anything
inside a reverb — which is how Reid draws it too, with the effect hanging off a
mixer that decides how much of it is heard.

</Try>

<Aside kind="why">

The metallic ring is not a flaw in the patch, it is the patch being honest.
Three delay lines at three constant times put three characteristic frequencies
in the tail. A spring reverb has exactly the same problem for exactly the same
reason: a spring carries three modes of vibration, so it has three frequencies,
and that is where its famous _boinggg_ comes from.

</Aside>
