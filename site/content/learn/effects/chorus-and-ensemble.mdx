A string machine starts from the weediest source in this whole tutorial: a
single divide-down sawtooth, which Reid describes as about as interesting as a
bunch of boring things on a very boring day. Switch its ensemble on and it is
lush. The trick is to fool the ear into hearing several performances of one note
by splitting the signal into paths, modulating the delay in each, and mixing
them back together.

<Patch id="effects/chorus" show={["mode", "rate", "depth", "mix", "width"]} />

<Try>

Press Play with **Design** on Juno and take **Mix** from 0 to 1. Zero is an
exact bypass — the pair of detuned sawtooths, and nothing else. One delay path
against the dry signal is the simplest chorus there is, and Reid says outright
that the ear is not really fooled by it.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to Ensemble while the note is holding. There is no gap, because a design
change crossfades inside the module over five milliseconds. Three delay paths
are modulated by one low-frequency oscillator at a third of a cycle apart, which
is the circuit inside the Eminent Solina and most of the Italian string machines
that followed it. On headphones it is audibly wider than Juno.

</Try>

<Try>

Switch to Dimension and bring **Width** down to zero and back. Roland's design
leaves the dry signal out of the output entirely, because at this width the
original would work against the impression of space rather than for it. Width
touches only the wet path: narrowing the effect must not narrow the source.

</Try>

<Aside kind="history">

Roland built the cheapest of these designs out of one square-wave oscillator and
three frequency dividers, rounded off with simple low-pass filters into
approximate sine waves. The point was cost — clocks and dividers were pennies —
but it had a second benefit the designers were actually after: the relationships
between those four modulating waves suppress the warbling that mars cheaper
circuits.

</Aside>
